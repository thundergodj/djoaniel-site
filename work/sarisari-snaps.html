// Case study 04 · Physical product · Parametric system

Nine frames. One rule. Photographs first.

SariSari Snaps began as a rough grey print carried up Mt. Pulag. Print failures, fit tests, colour trials, and one shared OpenSCAD model turned it into a magnetic photo-frame system with nine formats.

01 · The first object

The first prototype contained almost everything the system would remove.

It was rough, hollow, centrally branded, and wrapped in a decorative border. Before it could hold a photograph, the frame was already demanding attention.

The joke

The prototype went to Pulag as a toy for the gods. The joke gave the project life; the work that followed gave it rules.

02 · Material answered

The printer turned clean geometry into a physical argument.

Renders concealed the exact point where a design failed. The print bed exposed it: lifted corners, rough first layers, excess filament, and damaged edges—each weakness at full scale.

The bed showedThe footprint and edge behaviour were not abstract finishing details. They determined whether the frame printed cleanly.
The surface showedDecorative geometry amplified roughness instead of disguising it. Every extra gesture gave the material another place to fail.
The iteration changedThe object became quieter and the model more repeatable: fewer ornamental edges, clearer pockets, and dimensions that could propagate without redrawing each frame.
03 · One rule, nine frames

I stopped drawing products and built a rule.

One fixed square pocket became the module. Columns, rows, and orientation could change; spacing, edge conditions, and the lower personalisation zone stayed governed by one OpenSCAD model.

04 · The photograph changed the brief

The frame was not the subject.

The moment a real photograph entered the prototype, the hierarchy became obvious. The object needed no stronger identity—only a precise pocket, a reliable grip, a quiet boundary, and a small place for meaning beyond the image.

Hold the photograph precisely.Let everything else recede.

The rule behind every later decision
05 · Colour by elimination

Colour stayed only when the photograph came first.

The early palette was broad, saturated, and often glossy. Real images exposed what swatches could not: yellow dirtied pale areas, saturated edges pulled attention from faces, and gloss threw highlights across the print.

Five matte colours survived: White, Black, Sage, Blush, and Dusty Blue. Each worked only if the photograph still arrived first.

06 · Meaning within a boundary

Personalisation earned a boundary.

Text stays on one line below the photograph grid. Its available length follows the frame width and orientation. A small stamp can share the same centre line; neither is allowed to compete with the images.

07 · What held

One rule held across all nine formats.

The final test was consistency at scale: from Solo to Darling Dozen, more photographs, new orientations, and a larger footprint without inventing a new object each time.

The module heldEvery format grew by combining the same photograph pocket, not redrawing it.
The hierarchy heldThe lower zone carried personalisation while the photograph grid remained uninterrupted.
The principle heldAt every size, the frame held the memory without becoming its subject.

The system improved by subtraction: fewer colours, fewer arbitrary dimensions, fewer decorative gestures, clearer limits. The result is a frame present enough to hold the photograph and quiet enough to let the memory arrive first.
